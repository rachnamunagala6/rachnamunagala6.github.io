Henna designs are built on symmetry: one motif, repeated around a center until it becomes a pattern. Draw a line in the circle and it repeats the same way. I've started one for you.

const mirror = true;

// Every line you draw is repeated around the center
for (let k = 0; k < petals; k++) {
  for (let m = 0; m < (mirror ? 2 : 1); m++) {
    ctx.save();
    ctx.rotate(2 * Math.PI * k / petals); // turn to the next petal
    if (m) ctx.scale(-1, 1);              // flip for the mirror image
    drawStroke(points);                   // your line, unchanged
    ctx.restore();
  }
}

GSK · AI evaluation

Checking whether AI answers can be trusted

The problem

GSK was building GenAI apps for finance, analytics and legal teams. Before people could rely on them, every answer needed to be checked for made-up facts and for claims its sources didn’t support, and checking by hand was slow.

What I did

I built evaluation pipelines with RAGAS, DeepEval and MLflow that score answers on 5+ quality measures, so hallucinations and grounding gaps are flagged automatically.

The result

AI validation became 40% faster, and problems were caught more reliably before apps reached users.

Side project · Coffee Guide

Finding out why an AI answer is wrong

The problem

When a RAG app gives a bad answer, there are two possible causes. Search found the wrong guide section, or Claude used the right section badly. From the answer alone, you can’t tell which.

What I did

I built a way to run search on its own, without Claude, to see exactly which sections it found. To improve search, I split the guides by heading so each piece covers one topic, and added meaning-based search so “tastes like vinegar” finds the section on sour coffee.

The result

A bad answer can be traced to search or to the prompt in seconds, so each fix goes to the right place.

Lessons

from building AI and running the work behind it

GSK · Generative AI

What I learned testing AI at GSK

  1. 1

    Accuracy isn’t the finish line.

    When testing GenAI tools for finance, I saw AI produce correct numbers that still weren’t useful, because they missed the business context. A good answer also reflects what the user meant, applies the right filters, and explains how the result was reached.

  2. 2

    AI evaluation goes far beyond checking whether an answer is right or wrong.

    I worked with evaluation frameworks like RAGAS and DeepEval to assess faithfulness, groundedness and answer relevancy. One of my biggest takeaways was that AI quality has many dimensions, and no single metric tells the whole story.

  3. 3

    The best test cases come from how real users ask questions.

    Some of the most valuable issues I found weren’t code failures. They came from ambiguous questions, finance terms the model misunderstood, and missing context. That changed how I test: I write scenarios based on how real people phrase things, not just on what the system was built to handle.

  4. 4

    Building reliable AI is an iterative, cross-functional process.

    Working with engineers, business stakeholders and subject matter experts showed me how important it is to turn real business requirements into meaningful test scenarios. The goal is AI that works outside a controlled environment and that people can trust and use in their everyday work.

Apple · AI data & program management

What I learned working at Apple

  1. 1

    Data quality is just as important as model quality.

    Working in AI data annotation taught me that even the most advanced models depend on the quality and consistency of the data they’re trained and evaluated on. Small inconsistencies in labeling or interpretation can have a significant downstream impact on model performance.

  2. 2

    Human judgment is essential to building better AI.

    One of my biggest takeaways was that AI development takes more than algorithms. Human feedback, contextual understanding and careful evaluation are critical, especially in ambiguous cases where there isn’t always one obvious correct answer.

  3. 3

    Clear guidelines are the foundation of consistency.

    I learned how important it is to set well-defined annotation criteria and workflows. When multiple people interpret the same data differently, it creates inconsistencies that affect overall quality. Clear documentation, calibration and continuous feedback help reduce that variability.

  4. 4

    Scaling AI requires scalable processes, not just better technology.

    From a project management perspective, I learned that reliable AI systems need coordination, efficient workflows, quality checks and continuous process improvement. Even small inefficiencies become significant when working with large volumes of data.
